Question 1: River Forecasting Proposal

1. Architecture diagram

Proposed system. A river-graph space-time network. Every ten minutes it reads everything known at issuance time t0t_0 (24 h of levels at S1-S4 and T1, rainfall at R1 and R2, recorded releases, and the release schedule announced up to t0t_0), places these on a small directed graph that follows the river, and predicts the level at S1-S4 at t0+60t_0 + 60 min with a median and an 80% band. Temporal blocks (T) learn how each location responds over time. Spatial blocks (S) pass information downstream along the river, one hop per block. Four T-S pairs give the reach needed for the reservoir signal to arrive at S4 and for travel times of several hours to fit inside the model’s view.

Combining sources. All sources are resampled onto one 10-minute grid (rainfall summed over each interval, levels taken at the interval end, schedules held as step functions). Each source becomes channels of the node it describes: levels on S1-S4 and T1, releases and schedule on the reservoir node Res, and rainfall on every node. Channels that do not apply to a node are zero with a zero mask. A projection per node type (Wtype:ℝ14→ℝ64W_\text{type}: \mathbb{R}^{14} \to \mathbb{R}^{64}) maps these heterogeneous inputs to a common width, so a level sensor, the reservoir and the latent tributary node can share the same T and S blocks afterwards. The time axis is extended by six future positions (t0+10t_0+10 to t0+60t_0+60 min). There, only the announced release schedule is filled in. Every measured channel is masked, so future rainfall and future levels never enter the model.

2. Assumptions

Supplied facts (not assumptions). Levels at S1-S4 and T1 every 10 minutes. Rainfall at R1 and R2 with timestamps and locations. Recorded releases and announced schedules with announcement and effective times. Station locations, river connectivity and distances. The monitored tributary joins between S1 and S2 and the unmonitored one between S2 and S3. Travel times are not supplied and change with conditions. Future rainfall and levels are unavailable at issuance.

  • History window: 144 steps, t0−1430t_0 - 1430 min to t0t_0 (24 h).

  • Outputs: for S1, S2, S3 and S4, the median and the 10% and 90% quantiles of the level at t0+60t_0 + 60 min.

The 14 channels per node and step are: level value, level mask, level age, R1, R2, R1 mask, R2 mask, recorded release, release mask, scheduled release, schedule age, future flag, and time of day (sin, cos).

Alignment. Across time: every source is placed on the same 10-minute grid ending at t0t_0 (A1). Rain gauges with irregular stamps are summed into the grid intervals. A value that is missing or has not arrived is set to the last valid value with mask 0. Level age is recorded explicitly in minutes. For rainfall and actual release, the sequence of masks identifies gaps within the window, without an additional age channel. If there is no previous valid value, use zero with mask 0. Across locations: stations are not interpolated onto a regular spatial grid. Each location is a graph node and distance enters as an edge feature, so the uneven spacing is kept. Not available: rainfall and levels after t0t_0, schedule changes announced after t0t_0, and the actual releases after t0t_0.

4. Model computations and design justification

Notation. τ∈{t0−143Δ,…,t0+6Δ}\tau \in \{t_0 - 143\Delta, \dots, t_0 + 6\Delta\} indexes the 150 steps (Δ\Delta = 10 min). i∈Vi \in V indexes nodes. xi(τ)∈ℝ14x_i(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^{14} is the input. zi(ℓ)(τ)∈ℝ64z_i^{(\ell)}(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^{64} is the representation after block ℓ\ell. 𝒰(i)\mathcal{U}(i) is the set of nodes directly upstream of ii (edges j→ij \to i follow the flow) and ejie_{ji} the edge features.

Input projection. zi(0)(τ)=Wtype(i)xi(τ)+btype(i)+ϵiz_i^{(0)}(\tau) = W_{\text{type}(i)}\, x_i(\tau) + b_{\text{type}(i)} + \epsilon_i, where ϵi\epsilon_i is a learned node embedding.

T block (causal temporal convolution at each node, two layers with dilations d1,d2d_1, d_2): ui(τ)=zi(τ)+GELU(LN(∑k=02Wkzi(τ−kd))).u_i(\tau) = z_i(\tau) + \mathrm{GELU}\Big(\mathrm{LN}\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{2} W_{k}\, z_i(\tau - k d)\Big)\Big). It only looks backwards in time, so a representation at τ\tau never uses inputs after τ\tau. The four T blocks use dilations (1, 2), (4, 8), (16, 32), (64, 1), giving a temporal reach of 2×128+1=2572 \times 128 + 1 = 257 steps, more than the 150-step window.

S block (directed, edge-conditioned river convolution with lags): zi+(τ)=ui(τ)+GELU(LN(Wsui(τ)+∑j∈𝒰(i)gji(τ)∑δ=05Uδuj(τ−δ))),z_i^{+}(\tau) = u_i(\tau) + \mathrm{GELU}\Big(\mathrm{LN}\Big(W_s u_i(\tau) + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{U}(i)} g_{ji}(\tau) \sum_{\delta=0}^{5} U_\delta\, u_j(\tau - \delta)\Big)\Big), gji(τ)=σ(a⊤[uj(τ),ui(τ),eji]).g_{ji}(\tau) = \sigma\big(a^\top [u_j(\tau),\, u_i(\tau),\, e_{ji}]\big).

The six lag taps (δ\delta = 0 to 50 min) let a node read its upstream neighbour’s recent past, and the gate gjig_{ji} (a sigmoid per edge, not a softmax over all positions, so this is not attention) scales the message by the current flow state. Across the confluence edges (T1 to S2 and U to S3) a second, separate weight set carries a weak reverse message for backwater (A5).

Reach. Each S block moves information one hop. The longest path the forecast needs is Res to S1 to S2 to S3 to S4, four hops, so four S blocks are the minimum and are used. Temporal reach is the T-block reach plus up to 4×54 \times 5 lag steps from the S blocks, so any input in the window at a node upstream of a station (or at the station itself) can affect that station’s forecast. Information does not travel upstream, except across the two weak confluence edges, which matches the physics (A5).

Forecast mapping. For station s∈s \in {S1, …, S4} and horizon k∈{1,…,6}k \in \{1, \dots, 6\}, with ℓs(t0)\ell_s(t_0) the last valid level and σs\sigma_s the station’s training standard deviation of 1-hour changes: [m,u,v]=MLPs(zs(4)(t0+kΔ)),q̂0.5=ℓs(t0)+σsm,[m, u, v] = \mathrm{MLP}_s\big(z_s^{(4)}(t_0 + k\Delta)\big), \qquad \hat q_{0.5} = \ell_s(t_0) + \sigma_s m, q̂0.9=q̂0.5+σssoftplus(u),q̂0.1=q̂0.5−σssoftplus(v).\hat q_{0.9} = \hat q_{0.5} + \sigma_s\,\mathrm{softplus}(u), \qquad \hat q_{0.1} = \hat q_{0.5} - \sigma_s\,\mathrm{softplus}(v). Predicting the change from the last level makes “no change” the default and keeps the quantiles ordered.

Training objective. Pinball loss over valid targets, with the one-hour horizon weighted most: ℒ=1|Ω|∑(b,k,s)∈Ωwk∑q∈{0.1,0.5,0.9}ρq(ybks−q̂q,bks),ρq(r)=max(qr,(q−1)r),\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|} \sum_{(b, k, s) \in \Omega} w_k \sum_{q \in \{0.1, 0.5, 0.9\}} \rho_q\big(y_{bks} - \hat q_{q, bks}\big), \qquad \rho_q(r) = \max(q r, (q - 1) r), with w6=1w_6 = 1, w1..5=0.3w_{1..5} = 0.3 and Ω\Omega the samples, horizons and stations whose target is a valid measurement. Training samples are every 10-minute issuance time in the training years, built as of t0t_0 (A3). During training, random sensor gaps (10 min to 6 h, any node) and simulated reporting delays are applied so the model learns to forecast with incomplete data. Adam, early stopping on a later validation period, and a final test on the most recent period. Data are split by time, never at random, and each split contains flood events.

How each source reaches each forecast.

  • S1: its own history, the reservoir node one hop upstream (recorded releases and the announced schedule for the next hour), and rainfall (mostly R1).
  • S2: S1 and T1 one hop upstream (main river plus monitored tributary), the reservoir two hops upstream, and rainfall.
  • S3: S2 one hop upstream, the latent node U (driven by R2 rainfall), and everything upstream of S2 within three hops.
  • S4: S3 one hop upstream, then everything above it, reaching the reservoir in four hops.

Every recorded release in the 24-hour window can reach S4’s forecast, because four hops cover the path and the window is longer than the assumed travel time (A6). This does not mean a release made at t0t_0 changes S4 within the hour. Physically it only reaches stations whose travel time from the reservoir is under one hour, and the model has to learn which stations those are.

Physical assumptions versus learned mappings. Physical structure is built in: the graph follows the river and its direction (A5), the T blocks are causal, distance is an edge feature, the forecast is a change from the last observed level, and no future measurement can enter. Learned from data: travel times and how they change with flow (A7), attenuation of waves, rainfall-runoff response, the unmonitored tributary’s contribution, sensor biases and the uncertainty width. Mass balance is not enforced, because levels (not discharges) are measured and rating curves are not supplied.

Why this design. A temporal-only model cannot use upstream signals that have not yet reached a station, and a spatial-only model cannot represent travel delays. Alternating T and S blocks gives both, with a reach that is easy to check against the river. Attention would also connect distant stations, but with only seven nodes and a known physical topology the graph already provides every path needed, at lower cost, with a structure reviewers can audit. A purely physical hydraulic model would need cross-sections, roughness and the unmonitored inflow, none of which are supplied. Quantile outputs give the committee an honest band instead of a single number.

5. Operation and exceptional conditions

(i) Missing observations. QC rules (physical range, rate-of-change limit, flatline longer than 1 h) convert bad values to missing. A missing value is replaced by the last valid one with mask 0 and its age. Because training randomly hides inputs, the model learns to lean on neighbours (for example, S3 from S2 and U when S3 is down) and to widen its band when inputs are stale. If a station’s own level has been missing for over 6 h, its forecast is still issued (its last valid level is old) but flagged as “degraded”, and the band comes from the mask-conditioned quantile head. If all inputs for a forecast are missing, the service issues no forecast for that station rather than a fabricated one.

(ii) Delayed observations. Each forecast is built from a data snapshot taken at the cut-off. A value that has not arrived is treated as missing for that run (age channel set). When delayed data arrive, the forecast for the same target time is re-run immediately and re-issued with a new version number and “as-of” time. The earlier version is kept in the archive, so evaluation always uses the forecasts that were actually issued. Training simulates the observed delay distribution (A3) so the model is used to this situation.

(iii) Announced future releases. The schedule channel holds, for each 10-minute step from t0−1430t_0 - 1430 to t0+60t_0 + 60 min, the release that was announced to be in force at that step, using only announcements made at or before the time the sample represents (for future steps, at or before t0t_0). When several announcements cover the same step, the latest one made before t0t_0 wins. The schedule-age channel tells the model how recently it was confirmed. Because the six future positions carry the schedule, a release change starting at t0+20t_0 + 20 min can affect S1’s forecast if the model has learned that releases reach S1 within the remaining 40 min. Downstream stations are affected only through their upstream path, as in reality.

(iv) Scheduled versus actual releases. The past uses recorded actual releases (QactQ^\text{act}). The past schedule as it was known at each time is a separate channel, so the model sees how far actual releases have drifted from schedules and learns how much to trust the next hour’s schedule. Operationally, if the latest recorded release differs from the schedule by more than a set threshold (for example 10%), the system re-runs immediately and issues the forecast under the current actual release held constant for the next hour, alongside the schedule-based forecast, and flags the discrepancy to operators. An unannounced emergency release is therefore picked up in the first forecast cycle after its record arrives, which under A3 is usually within 20 minutes of the release.

Feasibility of running every ten minutes. The model has roughly 0.3 M parameters. One forecast applies the T and S blocks at 150 x 7 positions, about 2.5×1052.5 \times 10^5 multiply-adds per position, so roughly 3×1083 \times 10^8 in total. Even a conservative sustained rate of 10810^8 multiply-adds per second gives about three seconds of arithmetic, leaving substantial room within the 10-minute cycle for software overhead and data handling. This is an operation-count estimate, not a measured latency. Data ingestion, QC and assembly are simple table operations. Re-runs for late data or release discrepancies have the same model cost. A small server is a reasonable starting deployment, with latency checked on that server before operation and a persistence forecast published automatically if the model run fails.

Q2.1

Direction. Every 20° bin is populated: the smallest holds 0.0263 of the kept pixels and the largest 0.0820, against 0.0556 for a uniform distribution. The histogram is uneven (about a 3x spread between bins), but no direction is missing or rare. Mirroring every video would map each direction θ\theta to 180°−θ180° - \theta, and a 90° rotation would map θ\theta to θ+90°\theta + 90°. Both only move mass between bins that are all well populated. The L1 distance between the histogram and its mirrored version is 0.264, and 0.400 for its rotated version, the same order as its distance to a uniform histogram (0.220). With only 100 digits in 50 videos, each contributing a few straight segments, this level of unevenness is expected from sampling. So mirrored and rotated trajectories should already be familiar to a model trained on this data. Only the mirrored or rotated digit shapes are new.

Speed. A typical digit moves 3.32 px per frame (median). 80% of kept pixels lie between 2.14 and 4.12 px/frame and 99% are below 4.67. At 2× a typical digit therefore moves about 6.64 px between the frames the model sees, and at 3× about 9.95 px. Both are far outside the training range: only 0.0340% of kept training pixels move faster than 6 px/frame (the x-axis of Figure 1(b) stops at 6 px).

Overlap bias. Where digits overlap, Farneback sees one merged blob whose shape changes, so flow there is unreliable. I checked how much this matters by splitting the frame pairs with a connected-component test (n_blobs in pa2/data.py): 60.9% of kept pixels come from pairs where the two digits touch or overlap in at least one frame. Their median speed is 3.30 px/frame against 3.51 for pairs with separate digits, so overlap pulls speeds down only slightly. They also make up 60.4% of the slowest 10% of pixels, about their overall share, so they do not dominate the slow tail. Using only the pairs with separate digits, the direction histogram still covers every bin (0.0257 to 0.0818, L1 distance 0.109 from the full histogram). The bias is too small to change either answer: all directions are familiar, and 2× and 3× are not.

This makes the three tests a controlled experiment. Mirror and rotation hold the motion distribution fixed and change only appearance, so a failure there points to a motion rule tied to how digits look. Speed-up holds appearance fixed and changes only the motion, so a failure there points to unfamiliar motion or to limited reach. Each test changes one factor, so the cause of a failure can be read off.

2. Baseline: ConvLSTM

Model A (pa2/models.py, config A): pixel_unshuffle(x, 4) to 16 x 16 x 16, two ConvLSTM layers (64 hidden channels, 3 x 3, stride 1, padding 1, one convolution producing [i,f,o,g][i, f, o, g], no peepholes, zero initial states), a 1 x 1 convolution 64 to 16, pixel_shuffle(., 4) and a sigmoid. PyTorch default initialisation and bias=True everywhere. Trainable parameters: 480,784 (layer 1: (16+64)⋅256⋅9+256=184,576(16+64) \cdot 256 \cdot 9 + 256 = 184{,}576, layer 2: 128⋅256⋅9+256=295,168128 \cdot 256 \cdot 9 + 256 = 295{,}168, output: 64⋅16+16=1,04064 \cdot 16 + 16 = 1{,}040). The forward pass runs 9 steps: steps 1-5 read the input frames, steps 6-9 read the model’s own previous prediction (no teacher forcing, gradients flow through the fed-back frames), and the outputs of steps 5-9 form Ŷ\hat Y.

Training (pa2/train.py): Adam, learning rate 10−310^{-3}, no schedule, batch 32, 30 epochs, final-epoch weights kept. Each epoch shuffles the 8,000 training videos and draws one window per video (models A, B: t∈{1,…,11}t \in \{1, \dots, 11\} at 1×). The loss is the squared error averaged over pixels and valid target frames of each video, then over the videos in the batch. Seeds 0 and 1 control initialisation, shuffling, window and augmentation sampling. Validation (1×, t=1t = 1) is logged every epoch for monitoring only.

Q2.3

Reach of model A: 8 px per time step.

  1. Patch folding. pixel_unshuffle(x, 4) turns each 4 x 4 pixel patch into one cell of a 16 x 16 grid. Moving one cell means moving 4 original pixels (centre to centre). Folding itself moves nothing (convention).
  2. One 3 x 3 convolution (stride 1, dilation 1) mixes a cell with its 8 neighbours, so information moves at most 1 cell per convolution.
  3. Layer 1 at step τ\tau applies one 3 x 3 convolution to [uτ,hτ−1(1)][u_\tau, h^{(1)}_{\tau-1}]. The gates are pointwise, and the cell update cτ=σ(f)⊙cτ−1+σ(i)⊙tanh(g)c_\tau = \sigma(f) \odot c_{\tau-1} + \sigma(i) \odot \tanh(g) and hτ=σ(o)⊙tanh(cτ)h_\tau = \sigma(o) \odot \tanh(c_\tau) are elementwise, so hτ(1)h^{(1)}_\tau can depend on inputs at most 1 cell away.
  4. Layer 2 applies one 3 x 3 convolution to [hτ(1),hτ−1(2)][h^{(1)}_\tau, h^{(2)}_{\tau-1}]. Another cell.
  5. Output. The 1 x 1 convolution, pixel_shuffle and sigmoid do not move information.
  6. All paths within one step to x̂τ+1\hat x_{\tau+1}: from uτu_\tau: layer-1 conv then layer-2 conv = 2 cells. From hτ−1(1)h^{(1)}_{\tau-1}: the same two convolutions = 2 cells. From hτ−1(2)h^{(2)}_{\tau-1}: only the layer-2 conv = 1 cell. The recurrence does not add more within a step, because each state is used once per step. The longest path is 2 cells = 2 x 4 = 8 original pixels.

An autograd check (scripts/reach_check.py) confirms it: the gradient of one output cell with respect to uτu_\tau and hτ−1(1)h^{(1)}_{\tau-1} is non-zero only within 2 cells, and with respect to hτ−1(2)h^{(2)}_{\tau-1} within 1 cell. Over several steps reach adds up (8 px per step), but a digit’s new position must connect to its old position, held in the state, within one update. With Figure 1’s median of 3.32 px/frame, 1× motion is well inside 8 px, 2× (6.64 px) is near the limit, and 3× (9.95 px) exceeds it.

Model D is D_dil64, selected by the fixed validation score after the disclosed additional experiment. It keeps C’s 4x4 pixel fold, two 64-channel ConvLSTM layers, 3x3 kernels, 1x1 output projection, pixel shuffle, sigmoid and nine-step autoregressive rollout. Only the second layer’s dilation and padding change from 1 to 2. The first layer keeps dilation 1 and padding 1. D uses the same speed augmentation as C and no symmetry augmentation. With each seed, its initial parameter tensors exactly match C’s because dilation changes the spatial connections without changing the weight shapes.

Under the Q2.3 conventions, one folded-grid cell is four original pixels. The longest path from the current input or previous first-layer hidden state passes through the first convolution’s one-cell radius and the second convolution’s two-cell radius. Reach is therefore (1 + 2) x 4 = 12 pixels. The previous second-layer hidden state has a two-cell path, or eight pixels. Pointwise gates, the output projection and reshuffles add no reach. The autograd check in logs/reach_D_dil64.txt confirms radii of three cells for u_tau and h1_prev and two for h2_prev. Thus D has strictly larger reach than A (12 > 8 pixels), with no attention.

D has the same 480,784 parameters as A and C: 184,576 in layer 1, 295,168 in layer 2 and 1,040 in the output projection, below the 721,176 limit. Both final-epoch candidate runs are reused. This gives a closer C-to-D comparison than the original 78-channel candidate, although dilation also changes which intermediate locations are sampled.

Q2.17 (final verdict, at most 150 words)

The final model learned useful predictive structure, but a general motion rule is not established. Speed exposure accounts for most of the improvement: C reduces A’s fast-motion errors by about 36-37%. Refined D improves 3x error by a further 2.11% over C, with both seeds benefiting, and its wrapper guarantees symmetry consistency. Its forecasts now move and localize the digits, but remain blurred and unreliable through overlap and bounces. Original-speed error still exceeds A’s. A skeptic would need reliable trajectories and wall bounces across speeds, shapes and seeds, rather than consistency or background-dominated pixel MSE alone. One missing test is forecasting unfamiliar non-digit shapes with known trajectories. The additional D design followed earlier test analysis, so an untouched evaluation set would also strengthen the evidence.
